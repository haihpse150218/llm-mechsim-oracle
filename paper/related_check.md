## 1. Claims

### C1 (discussion.tex)
> Iterative repair with test feedback is well established \cite{chen2023selfdebug,xia2024chatrepair,olausson2024selfrepair}

**Olausson: SUPPORTED.** In their pipeline, failing unit tests produce error messages (compile or runtime errors, or an input whose output differs from the expected output). A model turns these into feedback, and the code is then repaired (Sec. 2, Sec. 3). They evaluate this on HumanEval and APPS with GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Code Llama.

### C2 (discussion.tex)
> and the quality of feedback is known to limit self-repair \cite{olausson2024selfrepair}.

**SUPPORTED.**
- The abstract puts it as a hypothesis: self-repair is held back by the model's ability to give feedback on its own code.
- Sec. 4.2 (RQ b) tests it by replacing the feedback with feedback from a stronger model (GPT-3.5 or GPT-4). Gains are larger than with self-repair.
- Sec. 4.3 (RQ c) uses human feedback for GPT-4. Repair success rises from about 33% to about 53% (1.58x).
- The Conclusion states that the bottleneck is feedback.

"Known to limit" is fair.

### C3 (related.tex)
> Olausson et al.\ \cite{olausson2024selfrepair} show that feedback quality limits self-repair, and that self-correction without external signal is unreliable \cite{huang2024cannot,kamoi2024selfcorrection}.

- **First clause: SUPPORTED** (same evidence as C2).
- **Second clause: PARTLY (grammar).** As written, the sentence reads as if Olausson et al. also *show* that self-correction without external signal is unreliable. They do not. Every one of their runs uses unit-test execution as an external signal, and they never test self-correction without one. The claim belongs to Huang et al. and Kamoi et al., whose papers were not checked here.

Proposed:
> Olausson et al.\ \cite{olausson2024selfrepair} show that feedback quality limits self-repair. Without an external signal, self-correction is unreliable \cite{huang2024cannot,kamoi2024selfcorrection}.

### C4 (related.tex)
> ConvCodeWorld \cite{han2025convcodeworld} varies the kind and amount of feedback in conversational code generation,

**PARTLY.** ConvCodeWorld builds 9 feedback settings from three dimensions (Sec. 3.1, 3.2, Table 1):
- compilation feedback, always present;
- execution feedback: none, partial test coverage or full test coverage;
- verbal feedback from GPT-4o: none, novice or expert.

"Kind" fits. "Amount" fits only the execution-coverage dimension. The verbal dimension is about *expertise or quality*, not amount. The paper frames its results by feedback type and combination (Sec. 5.2), not by amount.

Proposed:
> ConvCodeWorld \cite{han2025convcodeworld} varies the type and quality of feedback (compilation, execution with partial or full test coverage, novice or expert verbal) in conversational code generation,

### C5 (related.tex)
> and Pan et al.\ \cite{pan2025gfr} vary model strength in the generator, feedback and refiner roles.

**SUPPORTED** (abstract). The authors vary model strength in the generation, feedback and refinement roles on MBPP, APPS and DS-1000.

### C6 (discussion.tex), the main problem
> This contrasts with feedback designs that report only failing tests or error magnitudes, and with results suggesting that weaker models mainly benefit from more feedback \cite{han2025convcodeworld,pan2025gfr}. In our setting it was the \emph{kind} of information that mattered, not its amount.

**Han et al.: NOT SUPPORTED as phrased, and partly the opposite.**
- What Han et al. report: weaker models given enough feedback can beat state-of-the-art models that get no feedback (abstract; Sec. 5.2.2; Tables 4 and 5). They do not claim that weaker models gain *mainly* or *more* from feedback, and they do not frame the result as *amount*.
- They also note that more execution feedback can hurt some weaker models; for example, Qwen1.5-32B does best with expert verbal feedback alone (Sec. 5.2.1).
- Their *expert* verbal feedback is generated by showing the simulator the reference (ground-truth) code (Sec. 3.1.1). Appendix C.5 (Tables 11 and 12) measures how often that feedback mentions the reference code: 2.5% to 51% depending on the simulator.
- So their strongest feedback already carries reference knowledge. That matches our point rather than contrasting with it. A reviewer who knows ConvCodeWorld could flag this.

**Pan et al. (GFR): PARTLY (abstract only).**
- Their abstract says feedback helps *weaker generators* mainly on MBPP, while it helps consistently on APPS and DS-1000. The pattern is task-dependent.
- The variable they study is feedback *versus* none and the strength of the feedback model, not the *amount* of feedback.
- Their feedback is written by an LLM, so its information content is not controlled.

"Results suggesting that weaker models mainly benefit from more feedback" therefore overstates both papers.

Proposed rewrite of the last two sentences:
> This differs from feedback designs that report only failing tests or error magnitudes. It also refines earlier results: feedback lets weaker models match stronger ones \cite{han2025convcodeworld}, and its benefit depends on which model fills each role and on the task \cite{pan2025gfr}, but there the feedback content was not controlled. Notably, the strongest feedback in ConvCodeWorld is written with access to the reference solution \cite{han2025convcodeworld}. In our setting it was the \emph{kind} of information that mattered, not its amount.

Shorter alternative, if space is tight:
> This differs from feedback designs that report only failing tests or error magnitudes. Prior work found that feedback lets weaker models catch up \cite{han2025convcodeworld,pan2025gfr} but did not control what the feedback contains; in our setting it was the \emph{kind} of information that mattered.
